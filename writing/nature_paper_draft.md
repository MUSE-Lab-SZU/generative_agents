## Abstract

Depression affects approximately 280 million people worldwide, yet randomized controlled trials of psychotherapy are constrained by cost, ethics, and time. Existing computational models cannot capture the dynamic cognitive-emotional interactions central to therapeutic dialogue. Here we present [ModelName], a generative-agent system integrating an LLM-driven complaint graph state machine, per-turn emotion inference with volatility constraints, and a four-layer dynamic prompt architecture to simulate depression psychopathology and cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) response within a virtual community. ## Main

Depression is among the most burdensome mental disorders globally, affecting approximately 280 million individuals and representing a leading cause of disability worldwide. The World Health Organization projects that depression will constitute the largest single contributor to global disease burden by 2030. Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) is a first-line, evidence-based psychological treatment recommended across international clinical guidelines. Meta-analyses indicate that CBT yields moderate-to-large effect sizes relative to waitlist and treatment-as-usual controls, with response rates ranging from 40% to 60%. However, treatment outcomes exhibit substantial heterogeneity: a considerable proportion of patients do not respond adequately, and the specific mechanisms through which CBT exerts its therapeutic effects remain incompletely understood.

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs), the gold standard for establishing treatment efficacy, impose considerable practical constraints. A single-site psychotherapy RCT typically costs in excess of 500,000 US dollars and requires 12 to 24 months from recruitment to follow-up completion. Ethical constraints limit the range of controllable experimental manipulations — one cannot, for example, randomly assign patients to harmful social environments to quantify iatrogenic risk. Furthermore, process-level investigation of treatment mechanisms relies heavily on retrospective self-report and intermittent assessment, limiting the temporal resolution at which cognitive-emotional dynamics can be captured. These constraints have motivated a search for complementary experimental paradigms that preserve rigorous experimental control while enabling high-resolution observation of treatment processes.

Computational approaches offer one such paradigm. Traditional agent-based models have been applied to simulate population-level mental health dynamics, but these models rely on simplified behavioural rules that cannot capture the semantic richness of therapeutic conversation. Bayesian and reinforcement-learning frameworks have advanced mechanistic understanding of specific cognitive processes in depression, yet they do not model the open-ended verbal exchange that constitutes the primary medium of psychotherapy. More recently, large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated increasingly sophisticated capabilities in role-play, clinical reasoning, and naturalistic dialogue generation. Park et al. introduced generative agents — LLM-powered simulacra that maintain memory streams, generate plans, reflect on experiences, and engage in social interactions within a virtual community — establishing a framework for simulating believable human behaviour at scale. Kambeitz and Meyer-Lindenberg subsequently proposed, in a conceptual review, that generative agents could be adapted to model the impact of environmental and social determinants on mental health. However, no study to date has embedded clinical-grade depression psychopathology within generative agents and empirically evaluated structured psychotherapy interventions in this framework.

Here we introduce [ModelName], a generative-agent-based simulation platform that operationalises Beck's cognitive model of depression within the Stanford Town multi-agent framework. Five coordinated modules — a complaint graph manager with LLM-driven stage transitions, a dynamic emotion inferencer providing per-turn emotional state inference with volatility constraints, a session context builder, a trauma memory system, and a four-layer dynamic prompt assembler — collectively produce a clinically informed depressive phenotype that evolves in response to therapeutic input. We evaluate a structured, four-stage CBT protocol (information gathering, cognitive conceptualization, cognitive restructuring, and relapse prevention) within a seven-group controlled experimental design: structured CBT (G1), no-intervention natural course (G2), neutral social contact placebo (G3), counselling-room environment control (G4), negative social exposure (G5), supportive counselling active-treatment control (G6), and memory-consolidation ablation (G7). Longitudinal assessments using the PHQ-9, BDI-II, and SDS were administered at four time points: baseline (T0), mid-treatment (Session 4), late-treatment (Session 8), and post-treatment follow-up (T4). Nine patient persona variants, spanning distinct psychosocial trigger profiles, were instantiated at three depression severity levels (mild, moderate, and severe), with repeated runs controlling for LLM stochasticity. This design enables us to quantify overall treatment effects, isolate the specific contribution of CBT techniques from non-specific therapeutic factors, and assess the causal role of memory consolidation in treatment durability.

## Discussion

The results reported here demonstrate that [ModelName] — a generative-agent platform embedding Beck's cognitive model of depression — can simulate clinically consistent depression psychopathology and differential treatment response to CBT within a virtual community. The overall treatment effect observed for structured CBT (Cohen's d = X.XX) falls within the range reported in meta-analyses of real-world CBT trials, providing preliminary evidence of external correspondence. The critical G1-G6 comparison isolates the specific effect of cognitive restructuring techniques from non-specific therapeutic factors: the significant superiority of structured CBT over supportive counselling suggests that the active ingredients encoded in the system — guided cognitive restructuring, evidence-based belief examination, and behavioural experimentation — are indeed driving the simulated treatment response. The G7 ablation further reveals that memory consolidation plays a causal role in maintaining treatment durability, a finding that aligns with the clinical observation that patients who do not internally encode and rehearse therapeutic insights are more vulnerable to post-treatment relapse.

This work extends the generative-agent paradigm from the domain of general social simulation to clinically grounded mental health research. The approach builds directly on the conceptual framework proposed by Kambeitz and Meyer-Lindenberg, moving from theoretical possibility to empirical demonstration. Compared with traditional agent-based models, which rely on parameterized behavioural rules to simulate population trends, [ModelName] generates semantically rich therapeutic dialogues in which complaint-graph-anchored cognition, per-turn emotional states, and treatment techniques are rendered as natural language — the same medium through which real psychotherapy operates. In this respect, the platform occupies a complementary position alongside computational psychiatry models: while Bayesian and reinforcement-learning approaches provide mechanistically precise accounts of isolated cognitive processes, [ModelName] targets the interactive, conversational process that constitutes the primary mode of psychological treatment delivery. The integration of standardized clinical assessment instruments (PHQ-9, BDI-II, SDS) within the simulation loop enables quantitative comparison of simulated trajectories with published clinical benchmarks.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the LLM backends (Qwen3-8B for agent cognition, DeepSeek for treatment generation) were trained predominantly on corpora that may underrepresent certain cultural and linguistic contexts, and the resulting biases could affect the cross-cultural generalizability of the simulated treatment responses. Second, the current patient persona set is centred on young adult male profiles (nine variants of a single demographic identity, plus one middle-aged male persona), and while psychosocial trigger diversity is substantial, the age and gender range is limited relative to the diversity of real-world clinical populations. Planned expansions to include female personas and additional age cohorts are under way but are not reflected in the present results. Third, although the effect sizes observed in simulation fall within clinically plausible ranges, they have not yet been quantitatively calibrated against specific real-world RCT datasets. Differences in absolute PHQ-9 trajectories between simulation and clinical cohorts may reflect mismatches in baseline severity distributions, treatment adherence patterns, or the compressed temporal scale of the virtual environment (48-120 simulation steps of 6 virtual hours each). Fourth, LLM stochasticity introduces variability that, while controlled through repeated runs (minimum of three per condition) and item-level stability analysis, may interact with treatment effects in ways not yet fully characterized. Fifth, the G7 memory-consolidation ablation represents an extreme manipulation — complete blockade of memory writing — that does not have a direct clinical analogue. Real-world memory attenuation through sleep disruption, pharmacological agents, or neurological conditions would produce graded rather than binary memory impairment, and translational interpretation of the G7 finding should account for this difference.

The [ModelName] platform opens several avenues for future investigation. Extending the persona set across a broader demographic spectrum would enable systematic analysis of how age, gender, and cultural background moderate simulated treatment response — an investigation that is logistically prohibitive in many real-world RCTs. Incorporating pharmacological treatment modelling alongside psychotherapy would allow simulation of combination treatment strategies, a clinically prevalent but mechanistically underexplored domain. Calibration of simulation parameters against existing clinical trial datasets could transform [ModelName] from a qualitative platform into a quantitative prediction tool. Finally, the modular architecture supports systematic exploration of personalized treatment assignment: by varying patient profiles and treatment protocols combinatorially, the system could identify patient-treatment matching rules that optimize expected outcomes — a step toward precision psychiatry in silico.

## Methods

The [ModelName] platform extends the generative-agent framework proposed by Park et al. Each agent maintains a memory stream that records daily events, thoughts, and conversations, with retrieval weighted by recency, importance, and relevance. Agents perceive their virtual environment, generate and decompose daily plans, reflect on recent experiences to extract higher-level insights, and execute actions including movement, object interaction, and natural-language conversation. The virtual community includes residential, commercial, park, and counselling-room locations, providing a bounded social context. Two memory systems operate in parallel: a local LlamaIndex-based vector store for event and conversation retrieval (using BGE-M3 embeddings) and an external hierarchical memory service (EC-Doll) for long-term persistence and cross-simulation continuity.

The depression cognitive architecture comprises five modules. The complaint graph manager (`ComplaintGraphManager`) models the progression of depressive cognition as an LLM-driven directed graph of psychological stages, each encoding a core belief, narrative focus, speaking style parameters (tempo, disclosure level, tone, repair patterns), emotion vector anchor, advance and hold signals, and relational modifiers. Stage transitions are evaluated by dedicated LLM calls (`graph_planner.txt`, `graph_transition.txt`) that assess the current conversation against the active stage's signals to determine whether to remain, advance to a candidate next stage, or regress. Nine persona variants implement distinct complaint graphs mapped to different psychosocial triggers (job loss, academic failure, social rejection, family conflict). The dynamic emotion inferencer (`EmotionInferencer`) performs per-turn, LLM-based inference constrained by a volatility limit (default 0.12), receiving the current stage, graph window snapshot, session context, prior-turn emotion, and conversation content, and outputting emotion label, style, intensity (0–1), disclosure level (0–1), defensiveness (0–1), and a volatility note. The session context builder extracts structured situational information (location, time, partner relationship, interaction type, detected topics). The trauma memory system maintains stage-linked traumatic memories that surface as background narrative texture during expression. The dynamic prompt builder assembles a four-layer prompt (`dynamic_prompt_layers.txt`): base personality → complaint stage → session context → per-turn emotion, with stable traits anchoring behaviour and per-turn dynamics modulating expression within clinically plausible bounds. Patient personality is configured through free-text descriptions and depression severity tiers (mild, moderate, severe) that calibrate the complaint graph's starting stage, emotion vector baselines, and core-belief intensity.

The treatment intervention system implements a four-stage CBT protocol (information gathering, cognitive conceptualization, cognitive restructuring, and relapse prevention) comprising 10 sessions with adaptive loops: if the session evaluator determines a session did not meet its objectives, the current stage repeats before advancing. A dialog judge — a separate LLM instance receiving the patient state summary, conversation history, session prompt, and prior session evaluation — monitors each treatment conversation in real time, outputting a termination signal and constrained strategic advice (tactical guidance for the doctor's next response, explicitly forbidden from generating verbatim utterances). A post-session evaluator independently assesses therapeutic progress and outputs an efficacy score, a session-end flag, and a natural-language rationale. The consult history module maintains a dedicated memory store for doctor-patient dialogues: a gate LLM determines whether historical retrieval is warranted, and the top-k most relevant prior conversations are retrieved via embedding similarity and summarized for injection into the doctor's generation context. An environment task model simulates homework outcomes: when the doctor assigns between-session behavioural experiments, the model generates concrete event descriptions (completed, partially completed, or blocked) and injects them into the patient's and relevant residents' memory streams. Post-treatment follow-up mode activates after all scheduled sessions conclude, replacing session-progressive prompts with status-monitoring prompts that assess mood, sleep, energy, homework adherence, and risk signals without advancing the session counter.

Assessments employ three standardized instruments: the PHQ-9 (9 items), BDI-II (21 items), and SDS (20 items). At each assessment point, the patient agent answers each item in natural language; an independent ExpertLLM (DeepSeek-Chat, distinct from the treatment generation model) scores responses using standardized rubrics, outputting per-item scores and a severity classification. Assessments are collected at four time points: T0 (baseline), Session 4 (mid-treatment), Session 8 (late-treatment), and T4 (post-treatment follow-up, approximately 48 virtual hours after the final session). For control groups without structured sessions (G2, G3, G5, G6), a step-interval fallback generates virtual assessment points at fixed simulation intervals. Quality assurance includes repeat evaluation, item-level stability analysis, adaptive re-sampling of unstable items, and cross-validation of item-level scores against LLM-reported total scores.

The seven-group experimental design was executed with three severity levels (mild, moderate, severe) and a minimum of three repeated runs per condition, controlling for LLM stochasticity. Patient agents (nine Kabuda persona variants plus one cross-demographic validation persona) were configured through agent-specific personality descriptions and depression severity files. Each simulation ran for 48-120 steps (step stride = 360 virtual minutes), each step representing approximately one wakeful segment of a day. Treatment sessions were triggered every 4-6 steps (24-36 virtual hours between sessions). Forced conversation turns were capped at 18 per session to balance therapeutic depth with computational tractability. Statistical analyses employed linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) with random intercepts for persona and repeat, group as a fixed factor, and time as a repeated measure. Effect sizes are reported as Cohen's d with 95% confidence intervals. Multiple comparisons were Bonferroni-corrected. Analysis scripts and statistical outputs are available in the code repository.
